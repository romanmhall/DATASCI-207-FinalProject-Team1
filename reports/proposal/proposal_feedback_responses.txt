QUESTION 1:
"We frame default prediction as binary classification" why binary?
Show that you considered the options and why you decided on this.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RESPONSE:

In designing our modeling strategy, we evaluated four distinct formulation 
paradigms before selecting binary classification:

1. Formulations Considered:

   a. Multi-Class / Ordinal Delinquency Classification (Roll Rates):
      - Framing: Predicting discrete delinquency buckets, such as Current 
        (0 Days Past Due), 30-59 DPD, 60-89 DPD, and 90+ DPD (Default/Charge-Off).
      - Evaluation: In consumer credit portfolios, extreme class imbalance 
        severely fragments intermediate delinquency states. While overall default 
        is 8.07%, intermediate stages (like 60-89 DPD) represent less than 0.5% 
        to 1.0% of all observations. Multi-class models on such fragmented 
        distributions suffer from severe minority-class degradation without 
        providing additional utility for an initial loan approval decision.

   b. Continuous Regression (Loss Given Default / Repayment Ratio):
      - Framing: Predicting the exact continuous recovery rate or net dollar loss 
        (Expected Loss = Probability of Default * Loss Given Default * Exposure).
      - Evaluation: Continuous recovery data requires post-default collections 
        tracking over multiple years. The Home Credit dataset captures origination 
        risk rather than post-charge-off liquidation proceeds. Furthermore, loan 
        origination policies require an immediate underwriting decision prior to 
        capital disbursement.

   c. Survival Analysis (Time-to-Default / Hazard Modeling):
      - Framing: Modeling time-to-delinquency using Cox Proportional Hazards or 
        accelerated failure time models to account for right-censoring.
      - Evaluation: While valuable for monitoring loan portfolio aging over time, 
        applicants in our primary dataset hold varied loan maturities, revolving 
        structures, and term lengths without uniform observation windows. 

   d. Binary Classification (Selected Approach):
      - Framing: Predicting TARGET in {0, 1}, where TARGET = 1 indicates that 
        the borrower experienced late payment delinquency (specifically 90+ DPD 
        on early installments), and TARGET = 0 indicates regular repayment.

2. Rationale for Selecting Binary Classification:

   - Alignment with Industry Underwriting Decisions:
     At loan origination, lenders must execute a discrete policy action: Approve 
     versus Reject (or referral to manual credit review). Binary classification 
     directly mirrors this operational decision gate.

   - Regulatory Standard (Basel II / III Accords):
     International banking frameworks define credit risk fundamentally around 
     the 12-month Probability of Default (PD), defined as a 90+ DPD delinquency 
     event. Framing the problem as binary classification allows our models to 
     directly output well-calibrated posterior probabilities P(TARGET = 1 | X).

   - Actionable Risk-Based Threshold Calibration:
     Probabilistic binary outputs enable lenders to move beyond arbitrary 0.5 
     cutoffs. By varying the decision threshold tau, we can construct cost-
     sensitive decision curves that reflect the real-world asymmetry of credit 
     risk (where the financial loss of a False Negative/Default is typically 
     5x to 10x greater than the opportunity cost of a False Positive/Rejection).

   - Dataset Ground Truth:
     The benchmark Kaggle dataset defines the primary label TARGET as a binary 
     indicator (8.07% positive default rate). Retaining this binary formulation 
     ensures direct comparability with established literature and public benchmarks.

Full Reference (APA 7th Edition):

   Berg, T., Burg, V., Gombovic, A., & Puri, M. (2020). 
   On the Rise of FinTechs: Credit Scoring Using Digital Footprints. 
   The Review of Financial Studies, 33(7), 2845-2897. 
   https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa014

Paper Summary & Relevance to Our Project (Appendix Context):

   - Published in The Review of Financial Studies (a premier finance journal), 
     this landmark study analyzes over 250,000 consumer purchases from a German 
     e-commerce platform.
   - The authors demonstrate that simple digital footprints (such as device type, 
     operating system, email provider, and time of day of transaction) predict 
     consumer default with an AUC of 0.696, rivaling the predictive power of a 
     traditional credit bureau score (AUC 0.683).
   - Most importantly, Berg et al. show that combining digital/behavioral signals 
     with traditional bureau data increases the AUC to 0.736, reducing default 
     rates by up to 40%. Crucially, the predictive gain was largest among 
     unscoreable and thin-file borrowers.
   - Our DATASCI 207 project directly builds on Berg et al.'s findings by testing 
     whether rich multi-table behavioral trajectories (installment repayment 
     gaps, previous application behaviors, and POS cash balance patterns) can 
     similarly substitute for or augment missing credit bureau records.

I wasn't familiar with the term "thin-file", maybe clarify.

RESPONSE:

Definition and Industry Context:

1. What is a "Thin-File" Borrower?
   A "thin-file" applicant is an individual who has little or no formal credit 
   history on record with major national credit bureaus (such as Equifax, 
   Experian, or TransUnion).

2. Technical Criteria:
   Under guidelines established by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau 
   (CFPB), an applicant is classified as thin-file if their bureau file contains:
   - Fewer than 1 to 5 credit accounts, or
   - Credit accounts that have been open for less than 6 months, or
   - No updated account activity reported to the bureau within the past 6 months.

3. Why Thin-File Status Creates a Structural Barrier:
   Standard credit scoring algorithms (such as FICO or VantageScore) require a 
   minimum depth of historical data (e.g., at least one account open for 6+ 
   months and recent reporting activity). Without this history, the algorithm 
   cannot compute a reliable score, rendering the applicant "credit unscoreable."
   
   In traditional banking systems, unscoreable or thin-file applicants are 
   routinely rejected outright or subjected to predatory interest rates, even 
   when they earn steady incomes and practice disciplined cash-budgeting habits. 
   This disproportionately affects young adults (students, recent graduates), 
   recent immigrants, gig-economy workers, and unbanked populations.

4. Operationalization in Our Project:
   In our dataset, thin-file borrowers are explicitly identified as applicants 
   who lack historical debt records in the supplementary bureau table (bureau.csv) 
   and exhibit missing values in traditional bureau inquiry metrics.
   
   Our central research goal is to demonstrate that multi-table behavioral 
   dynamics (such as installment repayment consistency from previous transactions) 
   can provide sufficient discriminative power to accurately evaluate the credit 
   risk of these thin-file borrowers, fostering financial inclusion without 
   sacrificing portfolio safety.
